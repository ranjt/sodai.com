Light: how we see things

★☆☆
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Explain how we see light sources and other objects.
  • Describe reflection, lateral inversion and refraction.
  • Describe how optical devices and the human eye work.

Close your eyes and you see nothing. Open them and the world appears, full of colour and detail. What is happening? Scientists have argued about this for more than two thousand years. Today we know the answer: we see when light enters our eyes.

Light sources

A light source makes its own light: the Sun, a candle flame, a light bulb, a torch, lightning and even a firefly (jonaki). Most things are not light sources. A book, a tree, your friend's face and even the Moon only reflect light that falls on them. The Moon looks bright at night because it reflects light from the Sun.

We see a light source when its light travels straight into our eyes. We see every other object when light from a source bounces off (reflects from) the object and then enters our eyes.

Stay safe

Never look directly at the Sun, even with sunglasses. Its light is strong enough to damage your eyes for ever.

Absorbed, reflected or refracted

When light hits a material, three things can happen. Dark, dull materials absorb most of the light, so they look dark. Light-coloured materials reflect a lot of light. Transparent materials such as glass and water let light pass through, but they can bend it (refract it).

Reflection and mirrors

A mirror is very smooth and shiny, so it reflects light neatly in one direction. A ray of light hits the mirror and bounces off at the same angle: the angle of the incoming ray equals the angle of the reflected ray, measured from a line at right angles to the mirror called the normal. Rough surfaces scatter light in all directions, so they do not make a clear image.

Your image in a mirror is the right way up but back to front. Raise your right hand, and the image raises its left. This is called lateral inversion. That is why the word AMBULANCE is painted back to front on the front of ambulances: drivers in front read it the right way round in their mirrors.

A periscope uses two mirrors at 45° to let you see over a wall or round a corner. Submarines use them to see above the water.

Refraction

Light travels more slowly in water and glass than in air. When light passes from one material into another at an angle, it changes direction. This is refraction. It makes a pencil in a glass of water look broken, and it makes ponds look shallower than they really are.

Optical devices

DeviceWhat it does to light
hand lensa curved glass lens refracts light to make things look bigger (magnified)
telescopelenses or curved mirrors collect light from far away so stars and planets look closer
pinhole cameralight travels in straight lines through a tiny hole to make an upside-down image
cameraa lens focuses light onto a sensor (or film) to record an image
kaleidoscopemirrors inside a tube reflect coloured pieces again and again to make repeating patterns

The human eye

Your eyelid protects the eye and spreads tears to keep it clean. Light enters through the pupil, a hole that gets smaller in bright light and bigger in the dark. The lens focuses the light to make a tiny upside-down image on the retina at the back of the eye. Millions of light-sensitive cells in the retina send messages along the optic nerve to the brain, which turns the image the right way up and tells you what you are seeing.

From the past

Around the year 1015, the scientist Ibn al-Haytham (born in Basra, worked in Cairo) wrote the Book of Optics. Through careful experiments, including with a darkened room and a tiny hole (a pinhole camera), he showed that we see because light enters our eyes, not because our eyes send out rays, as many people believed. He is often called the father of optics.

Healthy eyes in a screen world

★★★
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Use data from a survey.
  • Link eye health to the structure of the eye.
  • Plan a health campaign.
ScienceHealthMathsTechnologyWellbeing

More and more children around the world are becoming short-sighted (myopic): they see near things clearly, but far things look blurred, because the image forms in front of the retina instead of on it. Scientists estimate that by 2050 about half of the world's people could be short-sighted.

Research suggests that spending time outdoors in daylight helps to protect children's eyes. In Taiwan, schools encouraged children to spend 120 minutes outdoors every day, and the number of short-sighted children began to fall. Eye doctors also recommend the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes of close work or screen use, look at something about 6 metres (20 feet) away for 20 seconds.

Food matters too. Without enough vitamin A, people can develop night blindness. Bangladesh runs a national Vitamin A campaign that gives capsules to young children, and vitamin A comes from foods such as spinach, carrots, mangoes, eggs and small fish like mola.

Based on research reported in the journal Ophthalmology (2016) and Taiwan school studies; Bangladesh National Vitamin A Plus Campaign.

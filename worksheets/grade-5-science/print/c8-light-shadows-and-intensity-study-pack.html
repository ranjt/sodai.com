Shadows and light intensity

★☆☆
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals

  • Classify materials as transparent, translucent or opaque.
  • Explain how shadows change and how light intensity is measured.

On a sunny afternoon your shadow stretches out long and thin across the school field. At midday it shrinks to a small dark patch around your feet. Why do shadows form, and why do they change?

Light travels in straight lines

Light travels from a source in straight lines. You can see this when sunbeams shine through a gap in the curtains into a dusty room, or when a torch beam cuts through mist. It is also why you cannot see round corners.

Transparent, translucent and opaque

  • Transparent materials let almost all light pass straight through, so you can see clearly through them: clear glass, clear plastic, water.
  • Translucent (partly transparent) materials let some light through but scatter it, so things look blurred: tracing paper, frosted glass, thin cotton cloth.
  • Opaque materials let no light through: wood, cardboard, metal, your body.

How shadows form

A shadow forms when an opaque object blocks light travelling from a source. Because light travels in straight lines, it cannot bend round the object, so a dark area is left behind it. A shadow has the same outline shape as the object, but it shows no colour or detail.

The closer an object is to the light source, the bigger its shadow, because it blocks more of the light. Moving the object further from the source makes the shadow smaller and sharper. Turning the object changes the shadow's shape.

Bangladesh connection

Shadow puppet shows use cut-out shapes held between a lamp and a cloth screen. In many places, including Bangladesh, the times of some daily prayers were traditionally worked out by measuring the length of shadows.

Shadows during the day

The Sun seems to move across the sky from east to west (really, the Earth is spinning). In the morning and evening the Sun is low, so shadows are long. At midday the Sun is highest, so shadows are shortest. Shadows always point away from the Sun, so they move round during the day. A sundial uses this to tell the time.

Measuring light intensity

Light intensity is how bright light is. It is measured with a light meter (or a light sensor on a tablet) in units called lux. A light meter gives a number, which is fairer and more accurate than saying "quite bright".

sunny day outdoors50 000–100 000 lux
cloudy day outdoors1000–10 000 lux

How scientists measured light

People once thought light arrived instantly. In 1676, the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer noticed that one of Jupiter's moons appeared from behind the planet later than expected when Earth was further away. He realised that light takes time to travel. In 1849, Hippolyte Fizeau in France measured the speed of light on Earth using a spinning toothed wheel and a mirror 8 km away. Today we know light travels about 300 000 km every second. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes to reach us.

A brighter, cooler classroom

★★★
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Use light intensity data.
  • Apply knowledge of materials to a design problem.
  • Weigh up costs and benefits.
ScienceDesignMathsEnvironmentEconomics

In Bangladesh, classrooms can get very hot from April to October. Teachers often close the curtains to keep out the heat and glare, but then the room gets dark and the lights must be switched on, which uses electricity. Some learners at the back cannot see the board clearly.

Experts recommend about 300 to 500 lux for reading and writing in classrooms. Too little light strains the eyes; direct sunlight on desks causes glare and heat. Good classroom design uses daylight wisely: light-coloured walls and ceilings reflect light around the room, translucent blinds let light in but block direct sun, and trees or shades outside block heat from the walls and windows.

Thinking and working like a scientist

★☆☆
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Ask questions that can be tested and choose a type of enquiry.
  • Plan a fair test, measure accurately and record results in tables and graphs.
  • Spot patterns and anomalies, draw conclusions and use models.

Scientists are curious people who ask questions about the world and then collect evidence to answer them. You already work like a scientist whenever you wonder "What will happen if…?" and then try it to find out. This toolkit is used in every science topic you study this year.

1. Ask a question

A good scientific question can be answered by collecting evidence. "Does warm water dissolve sugar faster than cold water?" can be tested. "Is sugar nicer than salt?" is an opinion, so it cannot be tested scientifically.

2. Choose a type of enquiry

Type of enquiryExample from Grade 5
fair testDoes the length of a ruler change the pitch of its twang?
observing over timeHow does a bean seed change as it germinates?
pattern seekingDo people with longer legs jump further?
identifying and classifyingWhich materials are transparent, translucent or opaque?
research (secondary sources)How did Ole Rømer find out that light takes time to travel?

3. Plan a fair test

In a fair test you change one thing, measure one thing and keep everything else the same. These things are called variables. If you change two things at once, you cannot tell which one caused the result.

Change (independent variable)the one thing you choose to change
Measure (dependent variable)what you measure or observe to see the effect
Keep the same (control variables)everything else that could affect the result

Write a prediction with a reason: "I think … will happen because …". Your reason should use science you know.

Repeat measurements (often three times) and work out the mean (average) to make results more reliable. A result that does not fit the pattern is called an anomaly (an odd result). Check it and measure again.

Remember

To find the mean: add up the results, then divide by how many results there are. (20 + 22 + 24) ÷ 3 = 66 ÷ 3 = 22.

5. Record and present

  • A table has clear headings with units, e.g. Time (s).
  • Use a bar chart when the thing you changed is in groups or words (types of soil, kinds of fabric).
  • Use a line graph when the thing you changed is a number that changes smoothly (time, temperature, distance).

6. Find patterns and conclude

Look for a pattern: "The longer the ruler, the lower the pitch." Then write a conclusion that answers your question, using the data as evidence, and say whether it supports your prediction. Finally, evaluate: How could you improve the investigation?

7. Use models

Scientists use models to explain things that are too small, too big, too fast or too slow to see: the particle model of solids, liquids and gases; a globe and torch for day and night; a wave diagram for sound. Every model is useful, but every model has limitations: it is never exactly like the real thing.

Key words
variable
something that can change in an investigation
fair test
only one variable is changed
prediction
what you think will happen, with a reason
reliable
results you can trust because they are repeated
mean
the average of a set of results
anomaly
a result that does not fit the pattern
conclusion
what the results show, answering the question
model
a way of representing an idea or object

Two Boses: scientists from Bengal

★★★
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Learn how real scientists work.
  • Link evidence, creativity and new instruments.
  • Reflect on sharing knowledge.
ScienceHistoryMathsLanguageEthics

Jagadish Chandra Bose was born in 1858 in Mymensingh, in what is now Bangladesh. He studied in Kolkata, Cambridge and London, then became a professor in Kolkata. In 1895 he showed that invisible radio waves could ring a bell and set off a small explosion in another room, one of the earliest demonstrations of wireless signals.

Later he asked a bold question: do plants respond to the world as animals do? Plant growth is far too slow to see, so he invented the crescograph, which magnified tiny movements about 10 000 times. His careful measurements showed that plants react to light, heat, touch and chemicals. Bose believed knowledge should be shared with everyone and was reluctant to make money from patents. He also wrote one of the first science fiction stories in Bengali, about a cyclone stopped by a bottle of hair oil!

Satyendra Nath Bose taught physics at Dhaka University from 1921. In 1924, in Dhaka, he wrote a new way of explaining light and sent it to Albert Einstein, who was so impressed that he translated it into German himself. A whole family of tiny particles, bosons, is named after him.

Based on biographies of J. C. Bose (Bose Institute) and S. N. Bose (University of Dhaka).

Bangladesh is a land of water: three great rivers (the Padma, the Jamuna and the Meghna), hundreds of smaller rivers, haors, beels and ponds, and heavy monsoon rain. Yet clean, safe drinking water is still hard to find in many places. Where does water come from, and where does it go?

Water changes state

Water can be a solid (ice), a liquid (water) or a gas (water vapour). Heating makes ice melt at 0 °C and makes water evaporate, or boil at 100 °C. Cooling makes water vapour condense into droplets, and makes water freeze at 0 °C.

The water cycle

  • Evaporation: the Sun heats water in seas, rivers and ponds. Liquid water turns into water vapour (a gas) and rises.
  • Transpiration: plants take up water through their roots and lose water vapour from their leaves.
  • Condensation: high in the sky the air is cooler. Water vapour cools and condenses into tiny droplets that make clouds.
  • Precipitation: droplets join together and become heavy, then fall as rain (or hail or snow).
  • Collection: rain runs over the ground into rivers and the sea, or soaks into the ground as groundwater. Then the cycle starts again.

The Sun's energy drives the cycle. The water on Earth today is the same water that dinosaurs drank!

Why the water cycle matters

The water cycle brings fresh water to the land. Plants need it to grow, animals need it to drink, and people need it for drinking, cooking, washing, farming and factories. Seas are salty, but evaporation leaves the salt behind, so rain is fresh. Ice in glaciers and snow on mountains store fresh water and release it slowly.

Did you know?

About 97 out of every 100 litres of water on Earth is salty sea water. Most of the rest is frozen. Less than 1 litre in every 100 is fresh water that we can easily use.

Fresh water in Bangladesh

Most of the year's rain falls in the monsoon (June to September). Many people drink groundwater from tube-wells. Human activities affect both the quantity and quality of water:

  • Pumping too much groundwater in Dhaka makes the water level under the city fall every year.
  • Factories, tanneries and sewage have polluted rivers such as the Buriganga.
  • In some areas, tube-well water contains arsenic, a poison that comes naturally from rocks underground.
  • On the coast, rising seas and storm surges push salt into ponds, rivers and soil.

Desalination: making salt water drinkable

Desalination removes salt from sea water. One way copies the water cycle: heat the salt water so that water evaporates, then cool the vapour so that it condenses as pure water. The salt is left behind. Another way, called reverse osmosis, pushes water through a very fine filter that stops the salt.

Protecting water

We can collect rainwater from roofs, fix dripping taps, never throw rubbish into rivers, and use less water at home. Some coastal villages use pond sand filters and rainwater tanks.

Too much water, not enough to drink

★★★
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Explain a real water problem using science.
  • Compare solutions using evidence.
  • Plan actions to protect water.
ScienceGeographyMathsHealthClimate change

In Satkhira and Khulna, in south-west Bangladesh, people are surrounded by water: rivers, shrimp ponds and the sea. But most of it is too salty to drink. Storm surges from cyclones such as Aila (2009) flooded ponds with sea water, and rising sea levels push salt further inland each year.

Many women and girls walk several kilometres every day to fetch drinking water. Some families collect rainwater from their roofs in big tanks during the monsoon. Villages also use pond sand filters, which clean water from a protected pond, and small desalination plants that use reverse osmosis.

Drinking salty water for a long time is bad for health, especially for pregnant women, whose blood pressure can rise.

Based on reports about coastal salinity in Bangladesh (WHO, UNICEF, Bangladesh research studies).

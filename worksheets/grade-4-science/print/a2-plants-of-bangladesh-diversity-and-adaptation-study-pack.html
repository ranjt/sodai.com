Plants of Bangladesh and how they survive

From the giant banyan tree in the village to the tiny duckweed floating on a pond, Bangladesh is green with plants. Plants give us food, wood, medicine, cloth and the oxygen we breathe. Every animal depends on them. Let's find out how many kinds there are, and how each one is suited to its home.

Flowering plants

Most plants you know are flowering plants. They grow flowers, which make seeds, often inside a fruit. Rice, mango trees, lotus, sunflowers, grass and the shapla (water lily, our national flower) are all flowering plants. Even grass and rice have flowers, but they are small and green.

Non-flowering plants

Some plants never make flowers. Ferns and mosses grow from tiny spores instead of seeds. Mosses grow in damp, shady places like the walls of old buildings. Conifers, such as pine trees, make seeds inside cones, not flowers.

Did you know?

Mushrooms are not plants! They belong to a different group called fungi. They cannot make their own food from sunlight.

Adapted to their homes

An adaptation is a feature that helps a plant survive where it lives. Bangladesh has many different habitats, and the plants in each one are adapted in different ways.

Mangroves: living in salty mud

In the Sundarbans, the tide covers the ground with salty water twice a day. The mud has very little air. Mangrove trees such as the sundari (which gives the forest its name) have breathing roots that poke up out of the mud like pencils to take in air. Some have prop roots that hold the tree steady in soft mud. Their leaves can get rid of extra salt.

Floating plants

Water hyacinth floats because its leaf stalks are swollen and spongy, full of air, like a float. Its roots hang down to take in minerals from the water. The lotus has a waxy leaf that water rolls off, and long hollow stems that carry air down to its roots in the mud.

Dry and windy places

A cactus stores water in its thick stem and has spines instead of leaves, so it loses very little water. Coconut and palm trees on the coast have bendy trunks and long, split leaves, so storms do not snap them.

Natural changes

Floods can drown plants or bring rich new soil. Droughts dry them out. Cyclones blow trees down, and salty sea water pushed inland by storm surges can kill crops. Some plants survive; others die and new kinds may take their place. Over time the mix of plants in a place can change.

Bangladesh connection

After Cyclone Sidr in 2007, about a quarter of the Sundarbans was badly damaged, but the mangroves grew back over the following years. The Sundarbans also protects villages behind it by slowing the wind and waves.

Try this at home

Look for moss on a damp wall or tree trunk near your home. Use a hand lens to find the tiny capsules on stalks that hold the spores.

Water hyacinth: a problem or a gift?

★★★
Grade 4
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Weigh up good and bad effects.
  • Link science to jobs and design.
  • Give a reasoned opinion.
ScienceEconomicsDesignEnvironment

Water hyacinth (kochuripana) came from South America more than a hundred years ago. It grows very fast in warm, still water. It can double in size in about two weeks, covering ponds, canals and rivers like a green carpet.

This causes problems. Boats get stuck, fish have less light and air, and mosquitoes can breed in the still water underneath. But people in Bangladesh have found clever uses for it. Farmers pile it up to make floating gardens (dhap) to grow vegetables during floods. It is used as compost and cattle food, and craft workers weave the dried stems into baskets, mats and furniture that are sold around the world.

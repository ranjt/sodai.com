Storms, cyclones and our changing climate

★☆☆

Bangladesh sits at the top of the Bay of Bengal, a warm sea shaped like a funnel. Several times a year, huge spinning storms called cyclones form over this warm water. Some of them travel north towards our coast. Learning how they form, and tracking where they go, saves thousands of lives.

How a cyclone is born

  • The Sun heats the sea. When the water is warmer than about 27 °C, lots of water evaporates.
  • Warm, damp air rises quickly. As it rises it cools and the water vapour condenses into huge clouds.
  • More air rushes in to take its place, and because the Earth is spinning, the air begins to spin too.
  • If the sea stays warm, the storm keeps growing: from a low pressure area to a depression, a cyclonic storm, and finally a severe cyclone.

Around the world

The same kind of storm has different names: a cyclone in the Bay of Bengal and Indian Ocean, a hurricane in the Atlantic, and a typhoon in the Pacific.

Why are cyclones so dangerous?

  • Strong winds blow down houses, trees and power lines.
  • Heavy rain causes floods and landslides.
  • A storm surge, a wall of sea water pushed onto the land by the wind, floods villages and makes the soil salty.

Tracking a cyclone

Weather satellites take pictures of the clouds every few minutes. Scientists mark the centre of the cyclone on a map using latitude (how far north) and longitude (how far east). Joining the points shows the cyclone's track and helps predict where it will reach land.

Warning signals

Bangladesh uses warning signals numbered 1 to 11 for the sea ports. The higher the number, the greater the danger. Signals 8, 9 and 10 mean great danger: people in the coastal areas must go to a cyclone shelter. Volunteers spread the warning with megaphones, flags and mobile phone messages.

Our changing climate

Burning coal, oil and gas in cars, factories and power stations, and cutting down forests, add more carbon dioxide to the air. It acts like a blanket and traps heat, so the Earth is slowly getting warmer. Warmer seas can make cyclones stronger and wetter, melting ice makes the sea level rise, and rainfall becomes harder to predict.

Bangladesh connection

Bangladesh produces only a tiny share of the world's carbon dioxide, but it is one of the countries most at risk from climate change, because so much of the land is low and close to the sea.

Ready for the storm: a family cyclone plan

★★★
Grade 4
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Use real warnings.
  • Plan to keep people and animals safe.
  • Think about helping others.
ScienceCitizenshipHealthMaths

Rahima lives with her family in a village near the coast in Khulna. One evening the radio says the signal has been raised from 4 to 7, and a cyclone may reach the coast in two days. Volunteers from the Cyclone Preparedness Programme walk through the village with megaphones and raise warning flags.

Rahima's family must decide what to take to the shelter, what to do with their goats and chickens, and how to help their grandmother, who cannot walk far. Thanks to warnings, shelters and volunteers, the number of people who lose their lives in cyclones in Bangladesh has fallen enormously since 1970.

Based on the Cyclone Preparedness Programme (Bangladesh Red Crescent Society and the Government of Bangladesh).

Why plants look the way they do

★★☆
Grade 4
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Explain adaptations.
  • Predict the effects of change.
1[4]
Look at the mangrove.
a)Why does the mangrove need roots that grow up out of the mud?
The wet salty mud has very little air, so the roots grow up into the air to breathe.
b)How do prop roots help in a cyclone?
They spread out like legs and hold the tree firmly in the soft mud, so the wind does not knock it over.
2[2]
Tisha says, "A cactus would grow well in the Sundarbans." Do you agree? Explain.
No. A cactus is adapted to dry places; in the wet, salty, muddy Sundarbans its roots would rot and it could not survive.
3[2]
A farmer's field near the coast is flooded by salty sea water after a storm. Predict what will happen to the rice and explain.
The rice will probably die or grow badly, because rice is not adapted to salty water; salt stops the roots taking in water.
4[3]
Design a plant that could live on a sunny, windy sand bank in a river (a char). Draw it and label two adaptations.
My plant
e.g. long roots to reach water and hold the sand; small waxy leaves to save water; bendy stem for wind.

Water hyacinth: a problem or a gift?

★★★
Grade 4
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Weigh up good and bad effects.
  • Link science to jobs and design.
  • Give a reasoned opinion.
ScienceEconomicsDesignEnvironment

Water hyacinth (kochuripana) came from South America more than a hundred years ago. It grows very fast in warm, still water. It can double in size in about two weeks, covering ponds, canals and rivers like a green carpet.

This causes problems. Boats get stuck, fish have less light and air, and mosquitoes can breed in the still water underneath. But people in Bangladesh have found clever uses for it. Farmers pile it up to make floating gardens (dhap) to grow vegetables during floods. It is used as compost and cattle food, and craft workers weave the dried stems into baskets, mats and furniture that are sold around the world.

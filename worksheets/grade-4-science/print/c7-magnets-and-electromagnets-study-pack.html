Magnets and electromagnets

★☆☆
Grade 4
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Observe how magnets behave and which materials they attract.
  • Describe magnetic poles and how magnets attract and repel.
  • Explain how electromagnets work and how magnets are used every day.

More than two thousand years ago, people found strange stones that could pull bits of iron towards them. They called them lodestones. Hung on a thread, a lodestone always turned to point north. These were the first magnets, and the first compasses helped sailors cross the oceans.

What do magnets attract?

Magnets attract (pull) things made of iron, steel (which contains iron), nickel and cobalt. These are magnetic materials. Most other metals are not magnetic: aluminium, copper, brass, gold and silver. Non-metals such as wood, plastic, glass and paper are not magnetic either.

Remember

Not all metals are magnetic! A magnet picks up a steel paper clip, but not an aluminium can or a copper wire.

Poles

Every magnet has two ends called poles: a north (N) pole and a south (S) pole. The magnetic force is strongest at the poles.

  • Unlike poles (N and S) attract each other.
  • Like poles (N and N, or S and S) repel (push each other away).

Magnetic force works at a distance

Magnetism is a non-contact force. A magnet can pull a paper clip without touching it, and the force passes through paper, plastic, water and even your hand. The space around a magnet where it has an effect is its magnetic field.

Strong and weak magnets

Some magnets are stronger than others. A stronger magnet can hold more paper clips, or pull a paper clip through more sheets of paper. Size does not always tell you the strength: some tiny magnets are very strong.

Earth is a giant magnet

The Earth behaves like a huge magnet. A compass needle is a small magnet that swings round to point north. Fishers on the Bay of Bengal, pilots and hikers use compasses to find their way.

Electromagnets

Wrap a long wire many times around an iron nail and connect it to a battery. Electricity flowing through the coil turns the nail into a magnet: an electromagnet. Switch off the electricity and the magnetism disappears. More coils or more cells make a stronger electromagnet.

Magnets in everyday life

compass
speakers
electric motors
electric bells
sorting cans
phones
MRI scanners
door catches

Fridge doors use magnetic strips to stay closed. Recycling plants use magnets to pull steel cans out of rubbish. Huge electromagnets on cranes lift scrap iron and drop it when the current is switched off.

Stay safe

Keep strong magnets away from phones, bank cards and computers, and never let small children swallow magnets.

Giant magnets in the ship-breaking yards

★★★
Grade 4
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • See how magnets are used in industry.
  • Use numbers.
  • Think about workers' safety and recycling.
ScienceEconomicsEnvironmentEthics

Along the beach at Sitakunda, near Chattogram, old ships from all over the world are taken apart in some of the biggest ship-breaking yards in the world. The steel from the ships is cut into pieces and recycled. Much of the steel used for building in Bangladesh comes from these old ships.

In steel yards and scrap yards, cranes with huge electromagnets lift piles of iron and steel. When the operator switches the current on, the scrap jumps up to the magnet; switching it off drops the scrap onto a truck. Ship breaking gives many people jobs, but it can be dangerous for workers and can pollute the beach and sea with oil and chemicals, so there are rules to make it safer and cleaner.

Based on reports about the ship-recycling industry in Bangladesh.

Habitats, food webs and our place in nature

★☆☆
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Compare the living things found in different habitats.
  • Use a food web to explain how energy and matter move through an ecosystem.
  • Evaluate how human activities affect ecosystems, and how nature inspires design.

A pond, a mango orchard, a tea garden in Sylhet, the coral reef at St Martin's Island and the mangrove forest of the Sundarbans are all habitats: places where living things find food, water and shelter. All the living things in a habitat, together with the non-living things around them (water, soil, light, air), make an ecosystem.

Biodiversity

Biodiversity means the variety of living things in a place. A rainforest or a coral reef has huge biodiversity. A field with only one crop has very little. Ecosystems with high biodiversity are usually healthier and recover better from problems such as disease or floods.

Food chains and food webs

Every food chain starts with a producer, a green plant or alga that makes its own food using light energy from the Sun. Consumers eat other living things: herbivores eat plants, carnivores eat animals and omnivores eat both. A carnivore that is not eaten by anything else is a top predator.

In a real habitat, most animals eat more than one kind of food, so food chains join together into a food web. The arrows show the direction in which energy moves: from the food to the animal that eats it.

Energy flows, matter cycles

Energy enters the ecosystem as sunlight and passes along the food chains. At each step, much of the energy is used for living (moving, keeping warm), so there is less for the next animal. That is why there are fewer tigers than deer. Matter is different: it goes round and round. When plants and animals die, decomposers such as fungi, bacteria and earthworms break them down, returning nutrients to the soil for new plants.

Living things depend on each other

Bees pollinate flowers; birds spread seeds; trees give shelter to monkeys and birds; crabs clean up dead leaves. When one species disappears, others are affected. Reproduction is important too: if a species cannot breed, its numbers fall and the whole web changes.

Humans and ecosystems

People change habitats by cutting forests, building, farming, fishing and polluting rivers and seas with chemicals and plastic. We can also protect them: national parks, tree planting, fishing rules and cleaner factories all help.

Learning from nature

Engineers often copy nature. This is called biomimicry. The hooks on burrs inspired Velcro. The shape of the kingfisher's beak inspired the quiet nose of Japan's bullet train. Termite mounds inspired buildings that stay cool without air conditioning. Lotus leaves inspired self-cleaning paints.

Bangladesh connection

Bangladesh has more than 700 species of birds, fish and mammals living in its rivers, haors, hills and mangroves. The Sundarbans is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and home to the Royal Bengal tiger.

The Sundarbans: a forest that protects us

★★★
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Explain why an ecosystem matters to people.
  • Evaluate the effect of a human activity, using evidence.
  • Argue for a way to reduce harm.
ScienceGeographyEconomicsEthicsClimate change

The Sundarbans is the largest mangrove forest in the world, about 10 000 km², shared by Bangladesh (about 60%) and India. Mangrove trees such as sundari and keora grow in salty, muddy water and have special roots that poke up out of the mud to breathe.

The forest is home to Royal Bengal tigers, spotted deer, crocodiles, river dolphins and hundreds of kinds of birds and fish. Hundreds of thousands of people depend on it for fish, crabs, honey and wood.

The Sundarbans also protects people. When cyclones such as Sidr (2007) and Aila (2009) struck, the mangroves slowed the wind and the storm surge, protecting villages behind them.

But the forest faces threats. In December 2014, an oil tanker sank in the Shela River inside the forest, spilling about 350 000 litres of heavy oil. Oil coated mud, roots and animals. Rising sea levels, more salt in the water, shipping, cutting of trees and poaching are other dangers.

Sources: UNESCO World Heritage listing; UNEP/UN assessment of the 2014 Shela River oil spill.

How heat travels

★☆☆
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Explain the difference between heat and temperature.
  • Describe conduction, convection and radiation using the particle model.
  • Explain how conductors, insulators and colours are used to control heat.

Hold a cup of hot tea and your hands warm up. Stand in the sun and your face feels hot. Heat is always on the move, from hotter things to colder things, until they reach the same temperature.

Heat and temperature

Heat is energy that moves because of a difference in temperature. Temperature (in °C) tells us how hot something is: how fast its particles are moving. A bucket of warm water holds more heat than a cup of boiling water, even though the cup is hotter.

1. Conduction

In a solid, heat passes from particle to particle. The hot particles vibrate faster and bump into their neighbours, passing the energy along. A metal spoon in hot tea soon feels hot at the handle.

Conductors let heat through easily: metals such as copper, aluminium and steel. Insulators do not: wood, plastic, cloth, wool and especially trapped air.

2. Convection

In liquids and gases, warm parts expand, become less dense and rise. Cooler, denser parts sink to take their place. This loop is a convection current. It stirs a pot of water on the stove and makes the daytime sea breeze.

3. Radiation

Heat can travel as invisible infrared rays, even through empty space. That is how the Sun's energy reaches Earth. Dark, dull surfaces absorb radiation well and heat up. Light, shiny surfaces reflect it.

Putting it together: a vacuum flask

The vacuum has no particles, so no conduction or convection can cross it. The shiny walls reflect radiation. The stopper stops warm air rising out by convection. The same flask keeps cold drinks cold!

Bangladesh connection

Traditional homes in Bangladesh use these ideas: thick mud walls and thatched roofs are good insulators, high ceilings let hot air rise away, and trees shade roofs from radiation.

Keeping cool in a hotter Dhaka

★★★
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Apply heat transfer to a real problem.
  • Compare solutions using data.
ScienceDesign and technologyHealthMaths

Many homes in Dhaka have thin tin (corrugated iron) roofs. On a sunny day the dark metal absorbs radiation and conducts heat straight into the room below. Inside, it can be much hotter than outside, which is dangerous for babies and older people during a heatwave.

Some families have tried cheap ideas: painting the roof white, adding a ceiling of jute or bamboo with an air gap, and growing creepers or trees for shade. A community group measured the temperature inside four rooms at 2 pm on the same hot day.

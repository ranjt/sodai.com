Systems, models and clever technologies from the past

★☆☆
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Explain what a system and a model are, and the limits of models.
  • Describe technologies from different civilisations.
  • Explain how past technologies still affect our lives today.

A bicycle, a beehive, the water cycle and the human body are all systems: sets of parts that work together and affect each other. Scientists and engineers use models to understand systems and to predict what will happen.

Systems

Every system has parts, connections between them, and often inputs and outputs. In a tube-well, the input is your push on the handle, the parts are the handle, rod and valve, and the output is water. If one part breaks, the whole system may stop working.

Models

A model is a simpler way of showing something real. A globe models the Earth; a map models a town; a diagram of the water cycle models how water moves; weather forecasters use computer models to predict cyclones.

Think like a scientist

Every model has limits. A globe shows the shape of the Earth but not its real size or the clouds moving over it. Good scientists always ask: "What does this model show well? What does it leave out?"

Technologies from past civilisations

  • Indus Valley (about 4500 years ago): cities such as Mohenjo-daro had planned streets, baked-brick houses and covered drains to carry dirty water away.
  • Ancient Egypt: the shadoof, a long pole on a pivot with a bucket on one end and a heavy weight on the other, lifted water from the Nile to the fields. It is a lever.
  • Persia (Iran): tall windcatchers caught the breeze and sent it down into houses to cool them, without electricity. Underground channels called qanats carried water across deserts.
  • China: paper, the magnetic compass and printing.
  • The Islamic world (around 1200): the engineer al-Jazari designed water-raising machines and clocks using gears and cranks.
  • Bengal: for centuries weavers made muslin, a cotton cloth so fine that a whole sari could pass through a ring. Ancient cities such as Wari-Bateshwar traded beads across Asia more than 2000 years ago.

From the past to the present

Today's sewers follow the same idea as Indus drains. Cranes use the lever and pulley ideas of the shadoof. Architects design new buildings with windcatcher-style towers to save energy. Old ideas often inspire new solutions.

Bringing back Dhaka muslin

★★★
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Explain how a past technology connects to today.
  • Consider history, science and economics together.
  • Form an opinion with reasons.
ScienceHistoryDesignEconomicsCulture

For hundreds of years, weavers in the Dhaka region made muslin, one of the finest cloths in the world. It was woven from a special cotton plant called phuti karpas, which grew along the banks of the Meghna river. Spinners twisted its short fibres into threads so thin they were almost invisible, often working in the damp morning air so that the threads did not break.

Muslin was traded to kings and queens across Asia and Europe. But in the 1800s, cheap machine-made cloth from factories, changes in trade and the loss of the skilled workers caused muslin making to die out. Even the phuti karpas plant was thought to be lost.

Recently, Bangladeshi scientists and weavers searched for the plant, compared its DNA with old muslin samples in museums, grew it again and trained weavers. In 2021 they succeeded in weaving new Dhaka muslin.

Based on news reports about the Bangladesh muslin revival project.

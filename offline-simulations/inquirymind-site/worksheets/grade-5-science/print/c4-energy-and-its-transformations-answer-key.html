Following the energy

★★☆
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Explain energy transfers.
  • Explain heating and cooling.
1[3]
Describe the energy changes when a rickshaw puller rides up a bridge and then freewheels down.
Chemical energy from food → kinetic energy of the rickshaw; going up, kinetic energy changes into (gravitational) potential energy; coming down, potential energy changes back into kinetic energy. Some energy is wasted as heat and sound.
2[2]
"When a ball stops bouncing, its energy has been destroyed." Do you agree? Explain.
No: energy cannot be destroyed; it has been transferred to the surroundings as heat and sound each time the ball hits the ground.
3[2]
Explain why a metal spoon in hot tea gets hot quickly, but a wooden spoon does not.
Metal is a thermal conductor, so heat moves through it easily; wood is an insulator, so heat moves slowly.
4[2]
A cold bottle of water and a hot cup of tea are left on a table. What happens to their temperatures? Explain using the word heat.
The tea loses heat to the room and cools down; the cold water gains heat from the room and warms up; both end at room temperature.
5[1]
Why is an LED bulb more efficient than a filament bulb?
It changes more of its electrical energy into light and wastes less as heat.

Power from the sun: solar home systems

★★★
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Trace energy transfers in a real system.
  • Use maths to plan energy use.
  • Weigh benefits and problems.
ScienceMathsEconomicsEnvironmentTechnology

Twenty years ago, millions of village homes in Bangladesh had no electricity. At night families used kerosene lamps, which give dim light, smoky air and a risk of fire. Since the early 2000s, millions of homes have bought solar home systems, making Bangladesh one of the world's biggest users of small solar systems.

A solar panel on the roof turns sunlight into electricity during the day. This charges a battery. At night the battery powers LED lights, a small fan, a phone charger and sometimes a TV. Children can study after dark, and shops can stay open longer.

Problems include the cost of buying a system, batteries that wear out after a few years and must be recycled safely (old batteries contain harmful lead or chemicals), and less power on cloudy monsoon days.

Based on reports about Bangladesh's solar home system programme (IDCOL, World Bank).
1[3]
Write the energy chain for an LED light powered by a solar home system at night.
light (Sun) → electrical (panel) → chemical (stored in battery) → electrical → light (+ heat) from the LED
2[4]
Energy maths. A 50 W panel gets 5 hours of good sunshine a day.
a)How many watt-hours (Wh) of energy can it make in a day? (W × hours)
50 × 5 = 250 Wh
b)A family uses 3 LED bulbs of 5 W for 4 hours. How much energy is that?
3 × 5 × 4 = 60 Wh
c)They also use a 20 W fan for 6 hours. Total energy used?
20 × 6 = 120 Wh; total 60 + 120 = 180 Wh
d)Is there enough energy left to charge a phone (10 Wh)?
Yes: 250 − 180 = 70 Wh left, more than 10 Wh.
3[2]
Give two ways solar lights are better than kerosene lamps.
No smoke, so healthier lungs; brighter light for studying; no fire risk; no fuel to buy; no carbon dioxide while working.
4[1]
Old batteries contain harmful materials. Suggest a way to deal with them safely.
Return them to the seller or a recycling centre so the materials are recovered and do not pollute soil and water.

Challenge. "We should save energy, but energy cannot be destroyed." Explain why both statements can be true.
Energy is never destroyed, but useful energy (like fuel or electricity) gets spread out as heat that we cannot use again; saving energy means using less of the useful stored energy (fuel), which is limited and costs money.

Every morning the Sun seems to rise in the east, climb across the sky and set in the west. For thousands of years people believed the Sun travelled around the Earth. Today we know it is the Earth that moves.

Day and night: the Earth spins

The Earth spins on an imaginary line through the North and South Poles called its axis. One complete spin (a rotation) takes 24 hours. The half of the Earth facing the Sun has day; the half facing away has night. Because the Earth spins from west to east, the Sun appears to move from east to west.

The Sun's path and shadows

In the morning the Sun is low in the east and shadows are long, pointing west. At midday the Sun is highest and shadows are shortest. In the evening shadows are long again, pointing east.

A year: the Earth orbits the Sun

The Earth travels around the Sun in a path called an orbit. One orbit takes about 365¼ days, which is one year. The extra quarter day adds up to one extra day every four years: a leap year with 29 February.

Why are there seasons?

The Earth's axis is tilted by about 23.5°. As the Earth orbits, the northern half is tilted towards the Sun for part of the year: sunlight hits it more directly, days are longer, and it is summer. Six months later it is tilted away: sunlight is spread out, days are shorter, and it is winter. When it is summer in Bangladesh, it is winter in Australia!

Places near the equator, such as Singapore, have about 12 hours of daylight all year and no real summer or winter. Places near the poles have the biggest changes: in summer the Sun never sets (the "midnight sun"), and in winter it never rises. Bangladesh is near the Tropic of Cancer, which actually passes through the country.

The Moon

The Moon orbits the Earth about once a month. It does not make its own light: we see it because it reflects sunlight. As it orbits, we see different amounts of its lit half, so its shape seems to change. These are its phases, from new moon to full moon and back in about 29½ days. The Islamic calendar follows the Moon.

The solar system

The Sun is a star. Eight planets orbit it: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. A sentence can help you remember the order: "My Very Easy Method Just Speeds Up Naming".

Stars and how scientists learned

Stars are huge, hot balls of gas, like the Sun but much further away. Groups of stars that make patterns are constellations. In Bangla, Orion is Kalpurush and the Great Bear's seven bright stars are Saptarshi Mandal. Around 1500 years ago the Indian astronomer Aryabhata suggested the Earth spins. In 1543 Copernicus argued that the Earth goes around the Sun, and in 1610 Galileo used a telescope to see moons orbiting Jupiter, which was strong evidence that not everything orbits the Earth.

Six seasons, one planet

★★★
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Compare seasons in different places.
  • Link seasons to culture and farming.
  • Explain using the Earth's tilt and position.
ScienceGeographyCultureMathsAgriculture

Bengali tradition divides the year into six seasons (ritu): Grishmo (summer), Borsha (monsoon), Shorot (early autumn), Hemonto (late autumn), Sheet (winter) and Boshonto (spring). Farming follows the seasons: Aman rice grows in the monsoon and is harvested in Hemonto, celebrated with the Nabanna festival of new rice, while Boro rice grows in the dry winter with water from tube-wells.

Other places have very different seasons. In Singapore, near the equator, it is hot all year and every day has about 12 hours of daylight. In Tromsø, Norway, far to the north, the Sun does not set for about two months in summer and does not rise for about two months in winter. In Sydney, Australia, south of the equator, December is the middle of summer.

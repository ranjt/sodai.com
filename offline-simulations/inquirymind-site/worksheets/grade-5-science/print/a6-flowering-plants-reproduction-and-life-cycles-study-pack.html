How flowering plants make new plants

★☆☆
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Name the male and female parts of a flower and say what they do.
  • Explain pollination, fertilisation and seed dispersal.
  • Describe the life cycle of a flowering plant.

Every spring the mango trees of Rajshahi and Chapainawabganj are covered in tiny flowers called mukul. A few months later the same branches are heavy with mangoes. Where did the fruit come from? It came from the flowers. Flowers are the parts of a plant that make seeds, and seeds grow into new plants. Making new living things of the same kind is called reproduction.

Inside a flower

A flower is like a small factory with different workers. The green sepals protect the flower while it is a bud. The petals are often brightly coloured and scented to attract insects.

The stamens are the male parts. Each stamen has a thin stalk called a filament holding up an anther. The anther makes pollen, a fine powder that contains the male cells.

The carpel is the female part, in the centre of the flower. At the top is the sticky stigma, which catches pollen. The style is a tube joining the stigma to the ovary. Inside the ovary are tiny ovules. Each ovule contains a female cell, the egg (ovum).

Pollination: moving pollen

Before a seed can form, pollen must travel from an anther to a stigma. This is called pollination. Plants cannot walk, so they need help.

Insect-pollinated flowers have large, bright petals, a sweet scent and sugary nectar. When a bee visits to drink nectar, sticky pollen rubs onto its hairy body. At the next flower, some of that pollen brushes off onto the stigma.

Wind-pollinated flowers, such as grasses and maize, are small and dull with no scent or nectar. Their anthers hang outside the flower and release huge amounts of light, dusty pollen. Their feathery stigmas catch pollen floating in the air.

Fertilisation: a seed begins

When a pollen grain lands on the right kind of stigma, it grows a thin pollen tube down through the style into the ovary. The male cell travels down the tube and joins with the female cell inside an ovule. This joining is called fertilisation.

After fertilisation, the petals wither and fall. Each fertilised ovule becomes a seed, and the ovary grows into a fruit that protects the seeds. A mango has one large seed; a tomato or a guava has many.

Seed dispersal: spreading out

If all the seeds dropped under the parent plant, the young plants would compete with their parent and with each other for light, water and space. So plants spread their seeds away. This is called seed dispersal.

  • Wind: light seeds with parachutes or wings, such as the fluffy seeds of the shimul (silk-cotton) tree, dandelions and sycamore.
  • Water: seeds that float, such as coconuts and the seedlings of mangrove trees in the Sundarbans.
  • Animals (eaten): juicy fruits such as guava and berries. Animals eat the fruit and drop the seeds in their droppings far away.
  • Animals (hooks): burrs with tiny hooks, such as chorkanta grass, catch on fur and clothes.
  • Explosion: pods that dry and burst open, flinging seeds out, such as peas and dopati (balsam).

The life cycle

A life cycle is the series of changes a living thing goes through in its life. For a flowering plant it goes round and round: a seed germinates, grows into a young plant, then an adult plant that flowers. Its flowers are pollinated, fertilisation happens, fruits and seeds form, the seeds are dispersed, and new seeds germinate.

Did you know?

Honeybees tell each other where to find flowers by doing a "waggle dance" inside the hive. The angle and length of the dance show the direction and distance of the food.

Bangladesh connection

In winter, the yellow mustard fields of Bangladesh buzz with bees. Many beekeepers move their hives to these fields. The bees collect nectar to make honey and, at the same time, pollinate the mustard so that the plants make more seeds for mustard oil.

Think like a scientist

Plants and animals depend on each other. Bees need flowers for food; many flowers need bees to carry their pollen. If pollinators disappeared, what would happen to the fruits and vegetables we eat?

Bees, mustard and honey

★★★
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Explain how plants and animals depend on each other.
  • Use data to compare results.
  • Consider different points of view and suggest action.
ScienceMathsGeographyEconomicsEthics

From November to January, much of northern and central Bangladesh turns bright yellow with mustard flowers. Farmers grow mustard for its seeds, which are pressed to make mustard oil for cooking.

Ayesha is a beekeeper from Sirajganj. Every winter she moves 40 wooden bee boxes to the edge of the mustard fields. "My bees collect nectar from the flowers and turn it into honey," she says. "The farmers like it too, because their mustard plants make more seeds when bees visit."

But there is a problem. Some farmers spray pesticides on their crops to kill pests. If the spray is used while the flowers are open and bees are flying, it can kill the bees too. Ayesha now talks to farmers so they spray in the late evening, when bees are back in their hives, or use other ways to control pests.

This story is based on real beekeeping in Bangladesh; the names and numbers are examples for learning.

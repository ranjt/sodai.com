Solids, liquids and gases: changing state

★☆☆

Wet clothes dry on the roof. A glass of cold water "sweats" on a hot day. Ice in a glass of lemon sherbet melts. A kettle whistles as water boils. All of these are changes of state, and every one is caused by heating or cooling.

Three states of matter

In a solid, particles are packed tightly in rows and can only vibrate, so a solid keeps its shape. In a liquid, particles are close together but can move past each other, so a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container. In a gas, particles are far apart and move quickly in all directions, so a gas spreads out to fill any space.

Evaporation

Evaporation is when a liquid turns into a gas. It happens at the surface of a liquid, at any temperature. Water evaporates faster when it is warmer, when it is windy, and when it has a larger surface area. That is why clothes dry fastest when they are spread out on a hot, windy day.

Condensation

Condensation is the reverse of evaporation: a gas turns into a liquid when it cools. Air always contains some invisible water vapour. When it touches a cold surface, such as a cold glass, a window in winter or grass at night, it condenses into drops. This is how dew forms on winter mornings.

Boiling

When water is heated strongly it boils at 100 °C. Bubbles of water vapour form all through the liquid, not just at the surface. While it is boiling, the temperature stays at 100 °C: the heat energy is being used to turn liquid into gas.

Melting and freezing

Ice melts at 0 °C, and water freezes at 0 °C. Different materials melt at different temperatures: chocolate melts at about 30–35 °C (in your hand!), candle wax at about 60 °C, and iron at over 1500 °C.

Evaporating a solution

If you dissolve salt in water and then let the water evaporate, the salt is left behind as crystals. The salt does not evaporate. This is how salt is made from sea water.

From the past

The Celsius scale was invented in 1742 by the Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius. Surprisingly, he first set 0 for boiling water and 100 for freezing water! It was turned the other way round after he died. In the USA people still often use the Fahrenheit scale, on which water freezes at 32 °F and boils at 212 °F.

Did you know?

On top of Mount Everest water boils at only about 70 °C, because the air pressure is much lower. It is hard to cook rice up there!

Salt from the sun: Cox's Bazar salt farmers

★★★
Grade 5
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Apply evaporation to a real industry.
  • Use data and maths.
  • Consider how weather and climate affect people.
ScienceGeographyMathsEconomicsHealth

Almost all the salt eaten in Bangladesh is made on the coast of Cox's Bazar and Chattogram. From about November to May, the dry season, farmers let sea water into shallow, flat fields covered with plastic sheets. The Sun and the wind evaporate the water, and white salt crystals are left behind. Farmers rake up the salt and carry it away in baskets.

In the monsoon, rain stops salt making: rain dissolves the salt and adds fresh water to the fields. Many salt farmers then grow shrimp or rice instead. Before it is sold, salt is cleaned and iodine is added, because our bodies need a small amount of iodine for healthy growth and brain development.

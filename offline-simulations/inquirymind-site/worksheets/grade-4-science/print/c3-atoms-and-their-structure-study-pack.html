Atoms: the building blocks of everything

★☆☆

Imagine cutting a piece of gold in half, then in half again, and again, and again. Could you go on for ever? About 2400 years ago, a Greek thinker called Democritus said no: in the end you would reach a tiny piece that cannot be cut. He called it atomos, which means "uncuttable". Today we know he was nearly right.

Everything is made of atoms

Your body, the air, the Sun, a mango, a mobile phone: all matter is made of atoms. Atoms are incredibly small. The full stop at the end of this sentence contains millions and millions of them. There are about 118 kinds of atom, one for each element.

Inside an atom

Atoms are made of even smaller particles:

  • The nucleus is the tiny centre of the atom. It holds protons and neutrons.
  • Protons have a positive (+) electric charge.
  • Neutrons have no charge (they are neutral).
  • Electrons have a negative (−) charge. They move very fast in the space around the nucleus.

Comparing the particles

ParticleWhere it isChargeMass
protonin the nucleuspositive (+)heavy (1 unit)
neutronin the nucleusnone (0)heavy (1 unit)
electronaround the nucleusnegative (−)very light (about 2000 times lighter than a proton)

An atom has the same number of protons and electrons, so the + and − charges balance and the atom has no overall charge.

Did you know?

An atom is mostly empty space! If the nucleus were the size of a marble in the middle of a football field, the electrons would be like tiny specks of dust flying around the edges of the stadium.

Different atoms, different elements

The number of protons decides which element an atom is. Every hydrogen atom has 1 proton; every carbon atom has 6; every oxygen atom has 8; every gold atom has 79.

Atoms join together

Atoms can join to make molecules. Two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom join to make one molecule of water (H2O).

Models help us think

Nobody can see inside an atom with their eyes. Scientists use models: drawings, balls and sticks, or computer pictures. Models help us understand, but they are not exactly like the real thing. Real electrons do not move in neat circles, and the nucleus is far smaller than in our drawings.

Try this at home

Make a model atom of carbon with clay or paper: 6 protons and 6 neutrons stuck together in the middle, and 6 electrons around them.

How did we find out about atoms?

★★★
Grade 4
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Learn how ideas change with new evidence.
  • Order events on a timeline.
  • Think about creativity in science.
ScienceHistoryMaths

Nobody has ever seen inside an atom with their eyes. So how do we know what atoms are like? Over hundreds of years, scientists did clever experiments and changed their models when they found new evidence.

In 1803, John Dalton in England said that each element is made of its own kind of atom, like tiny hard balls. In 1897, J. J. Thomson discovered the electron, so atoms could not be solid balls. In 1911, Ernest Rutherford fired tiny particles at a thin sheet of gold. Most went straight through, but a few bounced back! This showed that atoms are mostly empty space with a tiny, heavy nucleus. In 1932, James Chadwick discovered the neutron.

In a country of rivers, boats are everywhere: wooden nouka, river launches, fishing trawlers and huge cargo ships. Above them, kites, birds and aeroplanes fill the sky. How does a heavy steel ship float? How does a bird stay up? The answers are all about forces.

Copying nature

People have copied these features. Life jackets trap air like a duck's feathers. Boats have wide hollow hulls. Aeroplane wings are curved like a bird's. A parachute catches the air like a fluffy seed. Kites and gliders use the wind like soaring eagles.

The four forces of flight

  • Lift pushes the plane up. It is made by air flowing over and under the curved wings.
  • Weight (gravity) pulls the plane down.
  • Thrust pushes the plane forward. It comes from the engines (or a bird flapping its wings).
  • Drag (air resistance) pulls the plane back.

To take off, lift must be bigger than weight and thrust bigger than drag. When the forces are balanced, the plane flies level at a steady speed.

How things float

When you put an object in water, it pushes some water out of the way (it displaces water). The water pushes back up on the object. This upward push is called upthrust (or buoyancy). Gravity pulls the object down (its weight).

  • If the upthrust can become as big as the weight, the object floats.
  • If the weight is bigger than the biggest upthrust, the object sinks.

Density

Density tells us how heavy something is for its size. A stone is heavy for its size (dense), so it sinks. Wood is light for its size, so it floats. A steel ship floats because it is hollow: its shape is full of air, so the ship as a whole is light for its size and it displaces a lot of water.

The properties of air

  • Air takes up space and has mass.
  • Air can be squashed (compressed): we pump it into tyres and balls.
  • Air pushes on things (air pressure) and moves (wind).

We use these properties in tyres, balloons, life jackets, kites, sails, windmills and parachutes.

Bangladesh connection

In winter, Old Dhaka celebrates Shakrain with hundreds of colourful kites flying from the rooftops. Kite fliers use the wind to give their kites lift, and the string's tension to steer them.

Safe on the water: boats and launches

★★★
Grade 4
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Apply floating science to safety.
  • Use data about loads.
  • Suggest rules to save lives.
ScienceCitizenshipMathsGeography

Millions of people in Bangladesh travel by water every day: small nouka, ferries and big launches that carry hundreds of passengers between Dhaka and the south. When a boat is loaded, it sinks lower in the water. Every launch is allowed to carry only a certain number of people.

Sometimes, especially before holidays like Eid, launches are overloaded. The boat sits too low, waves can wash in, and it becomes unstable. In storms, overloaded boats can capsize. Safety rules say launches must not leave in bad weather, must carry life jackets and life buoys, and must not carry more people than allowed.

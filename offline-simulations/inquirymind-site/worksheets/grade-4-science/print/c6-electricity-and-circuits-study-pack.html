Electricity and circuits

★☆☆

Press a switch and the fan starts spinning. Plug in a phone and it charges. Electricity powers our homes, schools, hospitals and factories. But how does it get from the battery or the wall to the bulb? It needs a complete path called a circuit.

A complete circuit

Electricity flows from one end of a cell (battery), through wires and components such as a bulb, and back to the other end of the cell. The path must be complete, with no gaps. If there is a gap, the electricity stops and the bulb goes out. A circuit where everything is joined in one loop is a series circuit.

Circuit symbols

Scientists all over the world draw circuits with the same simple symbols, so everyone can understand them.

Conductors and insulators

Conductors let electricity flow through them easily. Most metals are good conductors: copper, aluminium, iron, steel, silver. Graphite (pencil "lead") conducts too. Insulators do not let electricity flow: plastic, rubber, wood, glass, dry paper and cloth.

Electrical cables have copper wires inside, because copper is an excellent conductor. They are covered in plastic to stop electricity reaching our hands. Plugs and switches have plastic cases for the same reason.

Switches and safety

A switch makes a gap in the circuit (off) or closes it (on), so we can control devices. Fuses and circuit breakers are safety switches: if too much electricity flows, they cut it off before wires get hot and start a fire.

Stay safe

Mains electricity from the wall socket (220 volts in Bangladesh) is about 150 times stronger than a 1.5 volt cell and can kill. Never touch sockets or switches with wet hands, never push anything into a socket, do not plug too many things into one socket, and never fly kites near power lines.

Electricity all around us

At home: lights, fans, fridges, pumps, phone chargers. At school: computers, projectors, bells. In the community: traffic lights, hospital machines, water pumps, street lights and mobile phone towers. Each is an electrical system: a source, wires, switches and components working together.

Lights for every home

★★★
Grade 4
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Use data about electricity in Bangladesh.
  • Think about electrical safety.
  • Plan how to save electricity.
ScienceHistoryMathsCitizenship

In the year 2000, only about one in three homes in Bangladesh had electricity. Children studied by the light of kerosene lamps, which were smoky and dangerous. Since then, new power stations, power lines and millions of solar home systems have brought electricity to almost every home in the country.

Electricity has changed lives: children can study at night, farmers use electric pumps, shops stay open and people charge mobile phones. But more electricity also means more need for safety and for saving energy, because much of it is made by burning gas and oil.

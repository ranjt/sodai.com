Wind, water and our changing land

★☆☆

Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals

Every year in Bangladesh, rivers eat away their banks and whole villages can fall into the water. At the same time, new islands of sand called chars appear in the middle of the rivers. The land is always changing shape. Moving water and wind are the main reasons.

Weathering, erosion and deposition

  • Weathering: rocks and soil are broken into small pieces by rain, heat, plant roots and ice.
  • Erosion: the pieces are carried away by moving water or wind.
  • Deposition: when the water or wind slows down, it drops the pieces in a new place.

Water changes the land

Fast-flowing rivers carry huge amounts of mud and sand. On the outside of a bend, the water flows fastest and wears away the bank. On the inside of a bend, and where the river meets the sea, the water slows down and drops the mud, building new land. Most of Bangladesh was built over thousands of years from mud carried by the Padma (Ganges), Jamuna (Brahmaputra) and Meghna rivers. Waves wear away coasts, and heavy rain can wash soil off bare hills and cause landslides.

Wind changes the land

Wind picks up dry sand and dust, especially where there are no plants. It can blow sand onto farmland and build sand dunes along beaches, like at Cox's Bazar and Kuakata.

Thinking in systems

A system is a group of parts that work together to do a job that no single part can do alone. A river is a system: rain, streams, the main river, its banks and the sea all work together to move water and mud. A coastal defence is a system too: a mangrove belt slows the waves, an embankment holds back the water, and sluice gates let rain water flow out but stop the sea coming in. If one part fails, the whole system is weaker.

Bangladesh connection

Bangladesh has built thousands of kilometres of embankments around low land near the coast, called polders. The Coastal Embankment Project began in the 1960s.

Living with a moving river

★★★
Grade 4
Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals
  • Understand river erosion in Bangladesh.
  • Use data about land loss.
  • Compare solutions from different viewpoints.
ScienceGeographyMathsEconomicsCitizenship

The Jamuna and Padma are among the biggest rivers in the world. Each monsoon they carry enormous amounts of water and sand. Their banks can collapse suddenly, and thousands of families lose their homes and fields to the river every year. People who lose their land often move to new chars or to the cities.

Engineers protect important places, such as towns and the Padma Bridge, with concrete blocks, geo-bags and walls along the banks. This is called river training. But it is expensive, and protecting one place can make the river push harder somewhere else.

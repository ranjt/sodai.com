Animals of Bangladesh: so many kinds!

Bangladesh is a small country, but it is full of animals. Tigers hide in the Sundarbans, dolphins swim in the rivers, hoolock gibbons swing through the forests of Sylhet, and every winter thousands of ducks fly in from the cold north. Scientists have counted more than 1,600 kinds of animals with backbones here, and many thousands of insects. This huge variety of living things is called biodiversity.

Where do animals live?

  • Aerial animals spend much of their life flying in the air: eagles, swallows, bats.
  • Arboreal animals live in trees: monkeys, gibbons, squirrels, many birds and snakes.
  • Aquatic animals live in water: fish such as the hilsa, Ganges river dolphins, crabs, frogs (for part of their life).
  • Terrestrial animals live on land: tigers, deer, elephants, cows and people.
  • Fossorial animals live under the ground: earthworms, moles, many ants and some snakes.

Two big groups

Scientists sort animals into groups by looking at their bodies. The first question is: does it have a backbone?

Vertebrates have a backbone and a skeleton inside their bodies. There are five groups: fish, amphibians (frogs, toads), reptiles (snakes, lizards, crocodiles, turtles), birds and mammals (tigers, bats, people).

Invertebrates have no backbone. Some, like insects, spiders and crabs, have a hard exoskeleton on the outside, like a suit of armour. Others, like earthworms and jellyfish, are soft. More than nine out of ten kinds of animals are invertebrates!

Skeletons inside us

Your skeleton is a strong frame of more than 200 bones. It supports your body, protects soft parts (your skull protects your brain, your ribs protect your heart and lungs) and helps you move. Muscles pull on bones to move them. Muscles can only pull, so they work in pairs: one pulls the bone one way, the other pulls it back.

Did you know?

Sharks have no bones at all! Their skeleton is made of cartilage, the same bendy material as the tip of your nose.

Branching keys

A key helps you name a living thing by answering yes/no questions about what you can see. Start at the top and follow the answers down the branches until you reach a name.

Adaptation

An adaptation is a feature that helps an animal survive in its habitat. The tiger's stripes hide it in tall grass. The Ganges river dolphin is almost blind, so it "sees" with sound (echoes) in the muddy water. Fishing cats have webbed toes for swimming. Birds' beaks are shaped for their food: a kingfisher's long sharp beak catches fish, a parrot's strong curved beak cracks seeds.

Migration

Migration is when animals travel from one place to another, usually at the same time each year, to find food, warmth or a safe place to breed. Every winter, ducks and waders fly thousands of kilometres from cold lands like Siberia to the haors and beels (wetlands) of Bangladesh. Hilsa fish swim from the sea up our rivers to lay eggs.

When habitats change

Natural changes, such as floods, droughts, cyclones and rivers changing course, can change a habitat. Some animals move away or die, while others find new homes. If a habitat changes too fast, a kind of animal may disappear from an area for ever. When the last one dies everywhere, it is extinct, like the dodo.

Bangladesh connection

Some animals that once lived in Bangladesh are now gone from here, such as the Indian rhinoceros and the wild water buffalo. The Royal Bengal tiger, the national animal, is protected in the Sundarbans.

Winter visitors: the birds of Tanguar Haor

★★★

Name:Class:Date:
Learning goals

ScienceGeographyMathsCitizenship

Tanguar Haor in Sunamganj is a huge wetland of lakes, marshes and swamp forest. In winter, when the water goes down, it becomes home to tens of thousands of migratory birds: ducks, geese, cranes and waders that fly from Siberia, Mongolia and the Himalayas. Because it is so important, it is a protected Ramsar site (a wetland of international importance).

Every year, bird scientists and volunteers count the birds. The counts show how healthy the wetland is. The birds face dangers: some people hunt them or catch them with poison bait, fishing nets can trap them, and too many boats disturb them. Local people, the government and nature groups work together to protect the haor.
